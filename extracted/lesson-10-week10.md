### Grammar — Plural Possessive Endings (on بيت / beet — "house")
| English | South Arabic | South Rom. | North Arabic | North Rom. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Our | بيتنا | beetna | بيتنا | beetna |
| Your (pl) | بيتكُم | beetkum | بيتكُن | beetkun |
| Their | بيتهُم | beethum | بيتُن | beetun |

*Note: "Our" (beetna) is identical in both South and North per source table. "Your (pl)" and "Their" differ (kum/hum vs. kun/un).*

### Grammar — Exception: "Dad" & "Brother" (South vs. Lebanese)
Base word أب (ab, "dad/father"):
| English | South Arabic | South Rom. | Lebanese Arabic | Lebanese Rom. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| My Dad | أبوي | abuwi | بَيّي | bayyi |
| Your (m) Dad | أبوك | abuuk | بَيَّك | bayyak |

Base word أخ (akh, "brother"):
| English | South Arabic | South Rom. | Lebanese Arabic | Lebanese Rom. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Her Brother | أخُوها | akhuuha | خَيّا | khayya |
| Our Brother | أخُونا | akhuuna | خَيْنا | khayna |

*Southern-primary rule: abuwi/abuuk/akhuuha/akhuuna are canonical for judging; bayyi/bayyak/khayya/khayna are the Lebanese-dialect accepted alternates (note: this is a third dialect variant beyond the usual N/S split, per the source's explicit "South / Lebanon" column headers — captured as its own "Lebanese" tag rather than folded into "North").*

### Example Sentence Cheatsheet — Lesson 1

**Possessives Pronouns:**
1. ahílkum jaayiin la-hoon? (S) — "Are your (p) parents coming here?"
2. wlaadhum naaymiin fis-sayyaara. (S) — "Their kids are sleeping in the car."
3. 3eelítna mín líbnaan. (N) — "Our family is from Lebanon."
4. ahílkun juwwa íl-beet? (N) — "Are your parents inside the house?"
5. madiintun zghiire. (N) — "Their city is small."

**Family Examples:**
1. ímmun mísh hoon. hiyye bísh-shíghíl. (N) — "Their mother isn't here. She's at work."
2. íbínkum fil-madrase? (S) — "Is your son at school?"
3. shu ísím bíntun? (N) — "What's their daughter's name?"
4. ukhtna saakne fi-madiinet 3ammaan. (S) — "Our sister lives in the city of Amman."

**Saying "Dad" & Brother:**
1. akhuuk hoon? la2, huwwa fíl-beet. **Standard** / khayyak hawn? la2, huwwe bíl-bayt. **Lebanese** — "Is your (m) brother here? No, He is at home."
2. abuwi mín suurya, bas ummi mín falasTiin. **Standard** / bayye mín libneen, bas ímmi mín falasTiin. **Lebanese** — "My dad is from Syria, and my mom is from Palestine." *(English gloss given only reflects the Standard line — see Extraction notes.)*
3. akhu íl-bínt 3aayísh fi-amriika.. ma3 abuuh. **Standard** / khay íl-bint 3aayísh bi-amriika ma3 bayyo. **Lebanese** — "The girl's brother is living in the USA with his dad."

**Together Examples:**
1. abuwi u-ummi mísh ma3 ba3D. (S) — "My dad, and mom are not together."
2. banaatna ma3 ba3D fil-madrase. (S) — "Our daughters are together at school."
3. hínne 3aayshiin ma3 ba3d. (S) — "They are living together."

### "Nice to Meet You"
| Slot | Arabic | Romanization | English | Literal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open | تشَرَّفنا | tsharrafna | Pleasure to meet you | "you honor us" |
| Response 1 | الشَّرَف إلي | ísh-sharaf íli | The pleasure is mine | "the honor to me" |
| Response 2 | تشَرَّفنا (أكتَر) | tsharrafna (aktar) | Pleasure to meet you (more) | "you honor us (more)" |

Note from source: the نا ("us") plural ending is used even if it's just one person, and the greeting does not change for feminine.

### "Goodnight"
| Slot | Arabic | Romanization | English | Literal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open | تِصبَح على خير | tíSba7 3ala kheer | Goodnight (m) | "may you wake up in goodness" |
| Response 1 | وإنت بخير | w-ínta b-kheer | Goodnight to you | "and you in goodness" |
| Response 2 | وإنت مِن أهلُه | w-ínte mín ahlu | And may you be good | "and you from it's family" |

Notes from source:
- To make the "Open" line feminine, use تِصبَحي (tísba7i); for plural, use تِصبَحوا (tísba7u).
- Response 2 is described as "the second and more poetic of the responses," with the beautiful literal meaning "and may you be from the family of goodness."

## Extraction notes / decisions
- This lesson introduces a third dialect tier beyond the usual North/South split: "Lebanese" forms for "Dad" and "Brother" possessives (bayyi/bayyak/khayya/khayna), explicitly tagged "Lebanon" in the source grammar table and "Lebanese" in the example-phrase/cheatsheet pages. These are captured as a distinct `Lebanese` dialect tag (not folded into "North"), since the source itself treats them as a separate column/category from the South/North contrast used everywhere else. South forms (abuwi, abuuk, akhuuha, akhuuna) remain canonical for judging per the project's Southern-primary rule; Lebanese forms are additional accepted alternates.
- Cheatsheet item 2 under "Saying 'Dad' & Brother" gives one English gloss ("My dad is from Syria, and my mom is from Palestine") that only matches the Standard line; the paired Lebanese line changes the content (dad is from Lebanon, not Syria) but the source only provides one translation for both. Recorded literally as given; the Extraction Phrases table above supplies a corrected/distinct gloss for the Lebanese line ("My dad is from Lebanon...") for clarity in app use.
- Spelling variants across pages for the same items (source inconsistencies, both forms recorded/accepted as matching):
  - "huwwe" (Example Phrases page) vs. "huwwa" (Cheatsheet) — "he," same word.
  - "bayyi mín líbnaan" (Example Phrases) vs. "bayye mín libneen" (Cheatsheet) — same phrase, vowel/spelling variant.
  - "abuuh" (Example Phrases) vs. "abuuh"/"abuuh" consistent; "bayyu" (Example Phrases) vs. "bayyo" (Cheatsheet) — "his dad," same word.
  - "bídha akíl" (Example Phrases, Lesson 2 Additional Vocab) vs. "bídda akíl" (Cheatsheet) — this is the same N/S "she wants" contrast documented in Lesson 6 (bídha canonical/Southern, bídda Northern accepted alternate).
  - "uuDa" is marked (N) and "ooDa" is marked (S) for "room" in the Lesson 2 vocab card; the cheatsheet/phrase examples use both uuDa- and ooDa- prefixed forms depending on which line is tagged (N) or (S) — consistent with the vocab card, not a new inconsistency.
